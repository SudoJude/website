## Project Overview

Fixperts pairs designers with someone who has a real problem and asks them to design a fix together. Max Gaynor and I worked with Julie and her daughter Jessica, who is my cousin. Jess has Down syndrome. She is non-speaking rather than non-verbal, she understands everything and communicates with Lámh signs and some signs of her own.

We started without a solution in mind. The result is a placemat with spinning, swappable picture wheels that lets Jess choose and communicate while she eats, and encourages her to practise speech. We documented the whole process in a short film.

## Exploration/Discovery

Our research plan focused on pain points, what already works, and opportunities for independence. Because Jess doesn't communicate verbally, we used two approaches, a semi-structured interview with Julie, and observing a session with Jessica's speech therapist, Susanna. We also read about Down syndrome and sensory processing.

The interview with Julie reframed the problem. Jess understands what is said to her and is very expressive, but others often miss her specific signs. The focus should be helping Jess communicate back. We then sat in on one of Jess's speech therapy sessions, where we learned that:

- Interaction needs to be slow, playful and full of positive reinforcement.
- Repetition locks in pronunciation, and tactile, visual and auditory cues help words stick.
- Picture cards help with word association.
- Speech improves when the environment makes talking necessary, rather than passive practice.

## Design Process

We turned this into a list of must-haves: interactive, visually intriguing, part of the daily routine, usable with other people, easy to carry, and something that encourages Jess to try to talk.

Our first thought was to put words or images on things Jess already uses, like plates and bowls. We sketched that idea across a toothbrush, lunchbox, water bottle, iPad, and clothing. Most of these either needed to be introduced into her life or were too specific to one place. We kept coming back to mealtimes, which happen everywhere.

This evolved into a placemat with moving parts, two removable circular wheels, one under the plate or bowl and one under the cup, each printed with images Jess can point to. The wheels swap or flip depending on the time of day, and could later cover activities as well as meals.

## Final Design

We built a first prototype and invited Julie and Jess to UL to try it. Before we had explained anything, Jess started pointing to the wheels and using them as intended, which told us we were on the right track.

We modelled the design in FreeCAD, which changed the proportions, the circles shrank to fit more naturally with Jessica's actual plates and cups. We cut the final pieces on a laser cutter in birch plywood, and backed the mat with rubber from a bathmat so it doesn't slide around. A groove down the middle lets a parent lift the wheels out and swap them.

For the symbols we considered burning them in with pyrography or inlaying coloured plastic, but settled on designing the wheels in Affinity, printing them on A3 and sealing the paper to the wood with a PVA and water mix. It was simple and gave a smooth, professional finish.

## Impact & Reflection

Julie and Jess both really liked the finished mat, and it worked as intended. Jess took to it straight away, as she had with the first prototype, which was the clearest sign that a tactile, playful object could fit into her mealtimes without being forced on her.

It's also easy to extend, new wheels can cover activities, tasks and other things Jess might want to ask for, as Julie suggested.
